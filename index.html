1

Field discovery: what galleries use, and why they say no

Before building further, I needed to see how galleries price and record their sales, and what would stop them from adopting anything new.

  • Cold email brought no conversions, so I moved to in-person visits and art-fair booth meetings, 14 of them at fairs.

  • From a list of 75 target galleries, I held 36 in-person meetings and moved 10 into PoCs.

  • I grouped galleries by the artists they represent, their size, and how often they exhibit, and changed the proposal for each group.

  • Among the galleries I surveyed, 20% kept records by hand and 80% used overseas SaaS.

  • The reason galleries gave for saying no: if their sales records get out, they lose negotiating power on price. That confirmed the constraint the product was designed around, which is that a gallery’s prices must never reach anyone else (section 4).

Data integration: an ontology built on one artist ID

    A price stored without its currency can be off by up to 1,300 times, so currency is resolved step by step (estimate text, then sale location, then a city currency table), and a record is stored only when a currency is found.   • Built

    Duplicate artists were merged by name normalization and image similarity, except where two records could be different people with the same name.

3

The pricing engine: anchor and tilt

A retrained gallery price model improved its overall score but got worse on the segment that mattered most.

Retrained modelBeforeAfter
Overall37.9%36.9%
Core segment28.7%38.3%
 Median absolute percentage error. Lower is better.

Instead of swapping in the new model, I compared where a model helps and where plain statistics do better, on the same test set. Gallery interviews and an auction-house seminar had shown me how prices are actually set: start from comparable works, then adjust for the differences.

Approach (same test set)Error
Model alone34.2%
Comparable sales alone32.0%
Anchor and tilt: comparables set the level, the model only adjusts23.9%
Same, with a medium-aware anchor23.4%
 Median absolute percentage error.
Anchor
The median price of comparable works by the same artist, within the same medium when there are 3 or more. It sets the price level, and it comes from statistics, not the model.
Tilt
How much this work’s size moves the price. The model runs twice, once at this work’s size and once at the artist’s typical size, and the ratio of the two is the tilt, kept between 0.5 and 2.0. Artist and medium effects cancel out in the ratio, so it still works for artists with little sales history.
Estimate
Anchor × tilt, returned with a calibrated range and a confidence grade.
  • BuiltUsed by galleries

    Pilot galleries set real prices with anchor and tilt estimates.

  • Built

    Ranges are calibrated. In testing, the 80% range contained the actual price 80.3% of the time. When comparable records are too few, the product shows no price rather than a weak one.

  • This isn’t a new algorithm. It automates the sales-comparison approach that appraisers use, with the model limited to adjustments.

4

One platform, an anchor per gallery

Each gallery prices the same artist a little differently, so one market-wide anchor couldn’t fit them all. At the same time, no gallery’s prices could ever reach another gallery.

Anchor swap
When a gallery has its own sales of an artist (same medium group, last 24 months), its anchor moves from the market median toward its own median. The weight grows with the number of its own sales, n/(n+5): with 4 sales, the anchor is 44% its own median and 56% the market median. The tilt stays shared, because a few dozen sales per gallery can’t train a model.
  • BuiltUsed by galleries

    Anchor swap. During the pilot it ran as a simpler rule that switched to the gallery’s own median once it had 3 sales of the artist; the weighted version above replaced it.

  • Built

    The estimate screen shows how each number was built: the artist’s typical price, how far this gallery’s own sales moved it, and the size adjustment.

  • Built

    Isolation is enforced in the database. Row-level security on 141 tables keeps each customer’s records inside its own scope.

5

On-site deployment, and field feedback into product

Galleries judged the product on their own works, so every demo ran on their own data.

  • Used by galleries

    On site, I entered a gallery’s own artworks or sales ledger and showed estimates on the spot. Pilot galleries then used these estimates when setting real prices.

  • I ran onboarding for the pilot galleries myself.

  • Built

    During the pilot, a client report took over 30 seconds. Timing each step showed repeated reads of the raw transaction table; precomputing those values brought it down to 0.76 seconds.

  • Built

    In the alpha test, a teammate ran scenario tests and logged 49 issues in a shared tracker. All 21 bugs were fixed. Of 28 issues where the product worked as designed but confused users, 26 led to changes (merged tabs, renamed labels, added guidance and fields), and 2 were kept.

  • Built

    AI proposes, people confirm. Image analysis fills 12 artwork fields only when 5 runs agree, and flags the rest for review. Generated drafts, email sorting, and collector suggestions never send anything or change data on their own.

  • Built

    Sales ledgers came in each gallery’s own format, so an LLM import reads the format as it is and asks the user only about rows with no currency.

Turning a law without final rules into a workflow

Korea’s artist resale-royalty law arrived before its rates and thresholds were final.

Suggestedthen Confirmedthen Artist notifiedthen one of PaidExemptDisputed
  • Built

    The rate (3%), threshold (KRW 500,000), and protection period (70 years) live in settings, not code. Each case stores the values in force when it occurred, so later changes never recompute past cases.

  • Built

    Detection creates a suggestion, not an obligation. Acquisition dates entered for admin purposes were being read as resales, so a person confirms each case.

  • Built

    The acquisition-date field tells users that filling it in makes the work subject to resale royalty.

  • Built

    In the same release, sales of KRW 10M or more create a reporting task due in 30 days, with the government form as a PDF. The threshold is borrowed from anti-money-laundering rules and marked as pending.
